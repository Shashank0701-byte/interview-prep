SCENARIO 1: BEHAVIORAL INTERVIEW - TECHNICAL LEAD ROLE
--------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: "Welcome! Let's start with a warm-up. Tell me about yourself and why you're interested in this role."

CANDIDATE: "I'm a senior software engineer with 7 years of experience building scalable backend systems and leading engineering teams. Most recently at CompanyX, I led the migration of our monolithic architecture to microservices, which improved deployment frequency by 5x and reduced infrastructure costs by 30%. I'm excited about this role because it combines my passion for technical architecture with team leadership, and I believe my experience building and scaling platform systems aligns perfectly with what you're looking for here."

INTERVIEWER: "Excellent, thanks. Tell me about a time you had to make a difficult technical decision with significant trade-offs."

CANDIDATE: "Sure. At CompanyX, we needed to choose between continuing with our SQL-based data warehouse or migrating to a NoSQL solution for our real-time analytics pipeline. We had 500GB of daily data and sub-second query requirements.

Here's how I approached it:

SITUATION: Our PostgreSQL-based analytics were hitting performance bottlenecks. Queries were timing out at 30 seconds, and the business wanted real-time dashboards.

TASK: I needed to decide on the data architecture that would support real-time analytics while maintaining data consistency and staying within budget.

ACTION: I ran a 2-week proof of concept comparing TimescaleDB (columnar PostgreSQL extension), ClickHouse, and Elasticsearch. I created a decision matrix with weighted criteria: query performance (35%), operational complexity (20%), cost (15%), team skill match (15%), and ecosystem maturity (15%). TimescaleDB won because it provided a 40x query improvement, seamless migration from PostgreSQL, and our team already knew SQL.

RESULT: We migrated in 6 weeks with zero downtime. Query latency dropped from 30s to under 200ms. The business got their real-time dashboards, and we saved $50K/year in infrastructure costs compared to alternatives."

INTERVIEWER: "Great approach. How did you get buy-in from stakeholders?"

CANDIDATE: "Three key strategies: First, I presented data from the POC — concrete numbers they could understand (40x faster, 40% cost reduction). Second, I created a risk matrix addressing migration concerns — data loss, downtime, team productivity during transition. Third, I proposed a phased migration: start with one non-critical dashboard, validate for 2 weeks, then roll out gradually. This gave stakeholders confidence and allowed us to adjust course if needed."

INTERVIEWER: "Now let's talk about people. How do you handle a situation where a team member consistently misses deadlines?"

CANDIDATE: "My approach is to diagnose rather than blame. First, I'd have a private 1-on-1 conversation exploring potential causes:

1. Is it a capacity issue? They might have too much on their plate.
2. Is it a skill issue? They might not have the technical skills to estimate or execute.
3. Is it a motivation issue? They might be disengaged or facing burnout.
4. Is it a process issue? Our sprint planning or requirements might be unclear.

In a recent case, an engineer was missing sprints consistently. Through our conversation, I discovered they were spending 3 hours daily on a legacy system integration that wasn't visible to the rest of the team. We reprioritized work, paired them with a senior engineer on the legacy integration, and within 2 sprints they were back on track. The key was addressing the root cause rather than just managing the symptoms."

INTERVIEWER: "How would you handle it if the issue was motivation rather than capacity?"

CANDIDATE: "Motivation issues are trickier. I'd start by understanding what's changed — is it personal circumstances, lack of challenging work, feeling undervalued, or misalignment with team goals? I'd use the 'Stay Interview' framework: What keeps you here? What would make you leave? What could we do differently?

If the root cause is lack of challenge, I'd look for stretch assignments, maybe leading a small initiative or mentoring a junior engineer. If it's recognition, I'd ensure their wins are visible in team meetings and leadership updates. If it's growth, I'd work on a development plan aligned with their career goals.

The most important thing is to be genuine and show you care about their success, not just their output. People can tell the difference."

INTERVIEWER: "Last question. How do you stay technically current while managing a team?"

CANDIDATE: "It's a challenge, and I'm honest about that. I use three strategies:

1. STRUCTURED LEARNING: I block 2 hours every Friday afternoon for technical reading, building small prototypes, or contributing to open source.

2. DELEGATED EXPLORATION: I rotate a 'tech scout' role on my team — each sprint, one person explores a new technology relevant to our stack and presents findings. This keeps the whole team learning.

3. HANDS-ON CODER: I still commit code, though less frequently. I focus on code review, architectural decisions, and picking up bugs in areas I want to learn about. Currently, I'm pairing with a team member on our Kubernetes migration to deepen my container orchestration knowledge.

I'm transparent with my team about my gaps and rely on their expertise too. Learning is a two-way street."

SCENARIO 2: TECHNICAL INTERVIEW - BACKEND ENGINEER
---------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: "Let's start with a coding problem. Design a URL shortening service like TinyURL."

CANDIDATE: "Let me start by clarifying requirements.

FUNCTIONAL:
- Generate a short, unique URL for any long URL
- Redirect short URLs to original URLs
- Optional: custom aliases, analytics (click tracking), expiration

NON-FUNCTIONAL:
- 100M new URLs per month
- 10K reads per second peak
- High availability (99.99% uptime)
- Low latency (<10ms for redirects)

Let me estimate scale:
- 100M new URLs/month = ~38 new URLs per second
- 1 year storage: 1.2B URLs
- 10K reads/sec, so read-to-write ratio ~ 260:1

For the encoding scheme:
- Base62 encoding (a-z, A-Z, 0-9) = 62 characters
- With 7 characters: 62^7 = 3.5 trillion unique URLs — more than enough
- Alternatively, we can start with 6 chars and increase as needed

For storage:
- Each row: 500 bytes (short key + long URL + metadata)
- Yearly: 1.2B × 500 bytes ≈ 600GB
- Cache: Redis for hot URLs, 80% cache hit ratio would serve 8K req/s from cache

High-level design:
1. Client sends POST /shorten with long URL
2. Server generates unique base62 ID
3. Store in database (key: short_url, value: long_url + metadata)
4. Return short URL to client
5. For redirect: GET /{short_url} → look up in cache/db → 301 redirect to long URL

Key components:
- Web server layer (auto-scaled)
- Redis cache cluster
- Database (distributed SQL like CockroachDB or NoSQL like Cassandra)
- Analytics pipeline (Kafka + streaming processor)

INTERVIEWER: "How would you handle URL collision in a distributed environment?"

CANDIDATE: "Several approaches:

1. DISTRIBUTED COUNTER: Use ZooKeeper or etcd for counter service. Assign each request an incrementing ID, then encode to base62.

2. ZOOKEEPER SEQUENCE: Each server gets a unique range of IDs from a centralized coordinator.

3. SNOWFLAKE ID: Generate unique IDs based on timestamp + datacenter ID + server ID + sequence.

4. RETRY ON COLLISION: Generate random keys and check for collision. With 62^7 keys, probability is extremely low, but we handle it with a retry mechanism.

I'd go with Snowflake IDs: unique, decentralized, and no single point of failure. We'd encode the numeric ID to base62 for the short URL."

INTERVIEWER: "Now implement a function to encode a numeric ID to base62."

CANDIDATE: [Writing code]
"Here's the Python implementation:

def encode_base62(num):
    characters = '0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyzABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ'
    if num == 0:
        return characters[0]
    
    result = []
    base = len(characters)
    while num > 0:
        rem = num % base
        result.append(characters[rem])
        num //= base
    
    return ''.join(reversed(result))

# Example: encode_base62(1223456789) -> 'aBc3xY'

And the decode function:
def decode_base62(short_url):
    characters = '0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyzABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ'
    base = len(characters)
    char_map = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(characters)}
    
    result = 0
    for char in short_url:
        result = result * base + char_map[char]
    
    return result

Time complexity: O(n) where n is the length of the short URL, which is O(log_base62(num)) for the ID."

SCENARIO 3: SYSTEM DESIGN INTERVIEW - REAL-TIME COLLABORATION
--------------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: "Design a real-time collaborative document editing system like Google Docs."

CANDIDATE: "Let me break this down.

REQUIREMENTS:
- Multiple users edit simultaneously
- Changes reflected in real-time (<500ms latency)
- Conflict resolution when users edit the same text
- History/versioning
- Offline support

KEY CHALLENGES:
- Consistency: How to merge concurrent edits
- Latency: Real-time updates across geographies
- Scalability: Millions of documents, thousands of concurrent editors per document

For conflict resolution, the two main approaches are:

1. OPERATIONAL TRANSFORMATION (OT):
- Used by Google Docs
- Each operation is transformed against concurrent operations
- Requires understanding of document state and operation semantics
- More complex but more expressive

2. CRDTs (CONFLICT-FREE REPLICATED DATA TYPES):
- Used by Figma, Notion, Apple Notes
- Data types designed to merge automatically
- Simpler to implement but higher storage overhead
- No central server needed for convergence

I'll go with OT as it's more battle-tested for text editing.

COMPONENTS:
1. Client (Browser): Rich text editor (Quill, ProseMirror, Slate) + OT client
2. WebSocket Server: Bidirectional real-time communication
3. Document Service: OT transformation engine + document state
4. Storage Layer: Document snapshots + operation logs (history)
5. User Service: Authentication, permissions, cursor presence
6. Version Database: MongoDB/Cassandra for document state + operation log

DATA FLOW:
1. User types → apply operation locally (optimistic)
2. Send operation via WebSocket to server
3. Server transforms operation against concurrent operations
4. Broadcast transformed operation to all clients
5. Each client applies the transformed operation

CONSISTENCY GUARANTEES:
- Eventually consistent across all clients
- All clients see the same final document state
- No user loses their changes

SCALING:
- WebSocket servers: horizontal scaling with sticky sessions
- Document partitioned by shard key (document_id)
- Redis pub/sub for broadcasting within document rooms
- Read replicas for document viewing (no write access)

FAILURE HANDLING:
- Client disconnects: buffer operations, replay on reconnection
- Server fails: migrate document room to another server
- Network partition: local operations queue, merge on reconnect
- Data corruption: version history for point-in-time recovery

INTERVIEWER: "How would you detect and resolve conflicting edits?"

CANDIDATE: "OT uses position-based transformation. Here's the key insight:

When two users insert at the same position, the server needs to adjust one operation so both are applied correctly.

Example:
- User A inserts 'X' at position 3
- User B inserts 'Y' at position 3 simultaneously

The server:
1. Receives both operations, sees they're concurrent
2. Transforms A's operation: 'insert X at 3' 
3. Transforms B's operation against A: 'insert Y at 4' (shifted by 1 because X was inserted)
4. Applies transformed versions to the document
5. Sends both users the final state

Both users see 'ABXY' instead of one overwriting the other.

The transformation functions (T functions) are the core of OT:
- T(insert(p1, c1), insert(p2, c2)) → if p1 < p2: return insert(p1, c1); else: return insert(p1 + 1, c1)
- For deletions: T(delete(p1, len1), insert(p2, c2)) → if p1 < p2: return delete(p1, len1); else: return delete(p1 + 1, len1)

This ensures all clients reach the same final state regardless of operation order."

SCENARIO 4: MACHINE LEARNING INTERVIEW - RECOMMENDATION SYSTEM
--------------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: "Design a recommendation system for a video streaming platform."

CANDIDATE: "Let me approach this systematically.

REQUIREMENTS:
- Personalized video recommendations for 100M+ users
- Real-time updates when users watch/interact
- Balance between relevance, diversity, and freshness
- Cold start for new users and new content

TWO-STAGE APPROACH:
1. CANDIDATE GENERATION: Retrieve hundreds of candidates from multiple sources
2. RANKING: Score and rank candidates using ML model

STAGE 1: CANDIDATE GENERATION

Source 1 - Collaborative Filtering:
- Matrix factorization (SVD, ALS) on user-item interaction matrix
- Items with similar interaction patterns to what user has watched
- Handles implicit feedback (watched, liked, shared, completed)

Source 2 - Content-based Filtering:
- Item embeddings from video metadata (title, description, categories, tags)
- Text embeddings from transcripts using sentence transformers
- Visual embeddings from thumbnails using CNN

Source 3 - Popularity:
- Trending content in user's region/demographic
- New releases with initial momentum
- Viral content

Source 4 - Social/Contextual:
- Friends watched, similar users watched (if social features exist)
- Time-aware: seasonal, time-of-day appropriate content

STAGE 2: RANKING MODEL

Architecture: Two-tower DNN model
- User tower: user history embeddings, demographic features, session context
- Item tower: content embeddings, popularity features, freshness signals

Features:
- User features: watch history (last 50 videos), genre preferences, session time, device, region
- Item features: content embedding, age (days since upload), completion rate, average rating
- Cross features: user-item interaction count, time since last watch of similar content, category match

Training approach:
- Implicit feedback (watched=positive, scrolled past=negative, completed=strong positive)
- Weighted logistic regression for probability of watch
- Top-k ranking loss to optimize for list quality

Evaluation:
- Offline: NDCG@k, Recall@k, Mean Average Precision
- Online: A/B testing with CTR, watch time, user retention, diversity metrics

INFRASTRUCTURE:
- Offline training pipeline: Spark for feature computation, TensorFlow for model training
- Online serving: TensorFlow Serving or TorchServe with <100ms latency
- Feature store (Feast/Redis): real-time features and precomputed embeddings
- Candidate retrieval: approximate nearest neighbor (Annoy, FAISS, HNSW)

CHALLENGES AND MITIGATIONS:

Cold Start (New Users):
- Ask preferences during onboarding (genres, languages)
- Use demographic-based recommendations initially
- Use exploration (epsilon-greedy, Thompson sampling)

Cold Start (New Content):
- Content-based recommendation using metadata similarity
- Temporary popularity boost for new items
- Random exploration for unseen content

Filter Bubbles:
- Inject diversity requirements in re-ranking stage
- Include serendipity exploration
- Balance personalized vs general recommendations

Scalability:
- Precompute candidates offline for active users
- Real-time inference only for ranking stage
- CDN cache for popular user segments

This two-stage architecture balances quality and latency, handling 100M+ users while keeping inference under 50ms."

SCENARIO 5: PRODUCT INTERVIEW - METRICS AND ANALYTICS
------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: "We've launched a new feature - collaborative playlists on our music streaming platform. How would you measure its success?"

CANDIDATE: "I'd structure this around three levels: adoption, engagement, and business impact.

LEVEL 1: ADOPTION METRICS (Did users find it?)
- Feature discovery rate: % of users who see the feature (impressions)
- Activation rate: % of users who create or join a collaborative playlist
- Time to first action: days between launch and first collaborative activity
- Adoption by segment: power users vs casual users, different age groups

LEVEL 2: ENGAGEMENT METRICS (Are users getting value?)

Primary:
- Collaborative playlist creation rate (weekly per user)
- Number of collaborators per playlist
- Songs added per collaborator
- Playlist completion rate (% of days playlist is played)

Quality signals:
- Song retention rate (tracks removed vs added)
- Collaboration recurrence (do they create more collab playlists?)
- User satisfaction survey (NPS for the feature)

LEVEL 3: BUSINESS IMPACT (Does this help the business?)

- Overall streaming minutes (do users stream more?)
- User retention (do users who use this stick around longer?)
- Social spread (invitations, shares, new user acquisition)
- Premium conversion (does this drive paid subscriptions?)

EXPERIMENTATION DESIGN:
- Randomize users into control (no feature) and treatment (feature available)
- Primary metric: weekly active users creating collaborative playlists
- Secondary metrics: total streaming minutes, social invites
- Guardrail metrics: playlist abandonment rate, app crash rate, latency

COHORT ANALYSIS:
Users who created ≥1 collaborative playlist vs those who didn't
- 7-day retention: 65% for collab users vs 40% for non-collab
- 30-day retention: 50% vs 25%
- Average session length: 45 min vs 30 min

SAMPLE SIZE CALCULATION:
- Minimum detectable effect: 5% improvement in weekly active use
- Statistical power: 80%
- Significance level: 5%
- Required sample: ~100,000 users per variant
- Duration: 2 weeks to capture weekly usage patterns

RISKS AND MITIGATION:
- Spam/inappropriate content: report and moderation system
- Privacy concerns: clear permissions for shared playlists
- Technical: latency for real-time sync across users
- Network effects: feature value increases with more users, so we need critical mass

I'd track these metrics weekly with a dashboard and make data-driven decisions on feature improvements: whether to invest more in discovery, engagement loops, or social sharing."
